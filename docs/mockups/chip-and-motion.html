Ludo · chip & motion design

The chip is the game

Everything a player feels comes through one object: a chip that reads instantly at 28 pixels, holds its color on any square, and moves with weight. This page pins down its shape, its states, how several chips share one square, and how it travels. Every motion demo below is live — tap the buttons.

01 · Form

Anatomy of a chip

A classic carrom-style pawn, lit from the upper left, with a hard white rim. Not cartoon, not glossy plastic — solid and legible. The rim is what keeps a blue chip readable on a blue square, which is where most Ludo apps fail.

Six seats. Colors are chosen so no two neighbours on the board are confusable, and so all six stay distinct for the ~8% of men with red–green color deficiency — seat order plus the rim carries the difference, never hue alone.
02 · Feedback

Chip states

A player should never have to guess which chips they may move. Legal chips announce themselves; everything else recedes.

03 · The hard part

Several chips on one square

This is where most Ludo apps turn into mush. The rule: up to two chips are drawn in full; three or more collapse into one chip plus a count badge. Same-color groups stack front-to-back; mixed colors sit side by side, because knowing whose chips are there matters more than how many.

Tap-to-expand. Tapping a stacked square fans the chips out in an arc above the board so each can be picked individually — the fan closes on selection or on tapping elsewhere. Below: closed, then fanned.
A pair is not a blockade. Both are two chips on one square, so they must not look alike. A blockade is drawn with a dashed wall around the square — it is a wall other players hit. A pair is drawn with a gold link and a ÷2 tag — it is two chips that have agreed to travel together, and only exists when the pair-move rule is switched on.
04 · Movement

How a chip travels

Movement is stepwise, never a slide: the chip hops once per pip, so a 5 visibly counts out five squares. Each hop is a 150 ms arc with a squash on landing, and the contact shadow shrinks as the chip rises. Six pips take about 0.9 s — fast enough not to stall the turn, slow enough to follow.

1.0×
Hop, don't slide

One arc per pip so the player can count the move as it happens. Sliding hides miscounts and feels weightless.

Capture is an event

The landing chip does a hard squash; the captured chip pops up, spins once, and arcs back to its yard. Roughly 0.7 s — long enough to register, short enough to forgive.

Home is a reward

Entering the home column slows down, the column lights up behind the chip, and the seat's counter ticks. Small confetti burst only on the fourth chip.

Illegal is instant

A blocked move never animates halfway. The chip nudges forward 4 px and stops, the blocking wall flashes, and the turn is still yours.

05 · Surfaces

The two boards

Four-player uses the classic 15×15 cross. Six-player uses a hexagon with triangular home bases, six arms and 78 track squares. Every yard holds four chips, whether or not you play with four. Chips shrink from 78% to 66% of a square on the hex board — the same chip, never a different design.

4 players · classic cross

52 track squares · 15×15 grid · square yards

6 players · hexagon

78 track squares · 6 arms at 60° · triangular yards

3 or 4 chips — the table decides. Six players × 4 chips runs long; 3 keeps a six-seat round near the length of a four-seat one. Neither is forced: chips per player is a setting on the rules screen, and the yard is drawn with four slots either way, so switching never changes the board.
06 · Themes

Light and dark, from day one

The app follows your system theme and can be overridden by hand in settings. Dark is not a filter over the light board — the surface, grid and yard interiors are their own palette. The chips are unchanged: their white rim is exactly what lets one chip design work on both grounds.
